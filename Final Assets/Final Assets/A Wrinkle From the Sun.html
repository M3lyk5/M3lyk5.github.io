WRINKLE FROM THE SUN



Images and description courtesy of Passenger Pigeon Press


After rain, a liturgy: she sits on the concrete slab by the pool rubbing Nair on her legs, pink foam oozing onto pavement, turning her towel into a sponge for rot. She feasts on Nair. The parking lot that surrounds the pool is covered in oil slick rainbows. She is often tempted by where black tar breaks and cracks, and where oil and rain has puddled together. Often, after this post-storm ritual of hair removal cream and watching the sky, she rolls up her shorts and stands in the shallow end of the pool to rinse off her legs. Today, she considers cleaning off the stench of Nair in an oil slick rainbow.

Because it is an unusually rainy spring, she has developed a maniacal obsession with rainbows. But her birthday is just around the corner, so she doesn’t believe in pots of gold. Instead, she prints out aerial photographs of the Deepwater Horizon oil spill and tapes them to her wall. Her diet is solely Skittles and all of her teeth fall out. The doodles in her textbooks look like this:

D(α) = (α - β) + (π - 2β) + (α - β) = π + 2α - 4β

Her dream dinner party guests are Newton, Descartes, and Leibniz arguing about who came up with the theory of refraction and raindrops and wavelengths; she was born in the wrong generation and is MAD about it. She exclusively wears the Balenciaga 2021 Pride collection. She listens to a crime podcast about the 1980 Rainbow Murders in Appalachia and figures out who the real killer is before the end of the first episode and receives a handwritten thank you from the man who was falsely convicted. She prays for tetrachromacy.

She grows suspicious of the artifices of the rainbow archive when every single major news outlet runs a story about a family where three of their four children are slowly losing their vision so their parents have allowed them to travel the world. The articles end with a photograph of the four siblings sitting on the roof of a camper van in Mongolia, their legs dangling off the side. The eldest brother’s hands are shoved in the pockets of his sweatshirt and he is beaming and proud. He is the only sibling who will not go blind. Behind them—which is presumably the reason for this photograph and a “must see” before sight vanishes—is a double rainbow. This also means that the reader must leave the story feeling hopeful. Instead, she wonders if the eldest brother feels guilty.

It has not rained in a while, and the oil slick rainbows have dried up. She is exhausted by rainbow mimesis. She spends her afternoons in churches lying under stained glasses, where everything is light and gauzy, like dust. She notices patchy hair on her legs and grows nauseous at the cyclical nature of time. The absence of a rainbow for 40 years begets the apocalypse. She falls in love with The Rapture. At night, the Nair can on her dresser winks in the dark.

She learns that rainbows are an unique interpretation of water and light that, even when two people are standing side by side, are experienced differently. They are rare and intangible. There is no universal “real.” She cannot make a distinction between a rumor and a rainbow.

The evidence is enervating: rainbow flora and fauna are usually poisonous. Tetrachromats are inherently selfish because they are more likely to have colorblind offspring. Children with vision impairments are reported to punch their fingers into their eyelids to produce color, so tetrachromats are also more likely to have children with a rainbow of bruises around their eyes. The chase to find the Rainbow Murderer led to the arrest of nine rural laborers in the area. Two witnesses were tortured by the police. The man who was falsely convicted was incarcerated for seven years. Can you taste it?

On T.V., the forecast finally declares rain. To practice for the aftermath, she watches a YouTube video of a double rainbow in Yosemite. On a camcorder, a man is out of breath, shaking, filming the lurid arc in the mountains beyond his front yard. He follows the bow from behind a tree, and when he steps out into a clearing and the rainbows are realized in their full form, he begins to scream:

Woah that’s so intense Oh my God
Wooooo
Wow

WOOOOO

Then, he starts to cry:

What does this mean It’s so bright
It’s so beautiful

He is sobbing/screaming/wheezing:

Oh my god
It’s a double completely right in my front yard
Oh my god
What does it mean
Tell me
It’s too much. Tell me what it means

She is stunned by this video because this is not her reaction to oil spills or endless cavities or exonerations. She waits for the forecasted storm and braces for tears. Like always, her legs are smooth and the oil slick rainbows are like ink on the pavement, stretching out and blooming. She is unimpressed. The next morning, she will run away to the desert where the world has turned beige and where her eyes will wrinkle from the sun. In the end, a confession:

I hate rainbows, and they have never moved me.


Martha's Quarterly, Issue 31, Spring 2024, Wrinkle From the Sun was produced using single-color photocopy, xerox, and laser cutting. Futura Medium font was used in various sizes and styles. The cover paper printed on Springhill blue 147 gsm cardstock. The internal pamphlets were printed on 20 lb. Hammermill green paper and 24 lb. Astrobright neon yellow paper.  The paper sleeve was printed on 20 lb. Neenah solar yellow paper. The pamphlet entitled “The Body at Work: A Treatise on the Principles of Physiology, Chapter XIII: Vision” was written by Alex Hill, M.A., M.D., F.R.C.S. and sourced from Project Gutenberg. Mely Kornfeld wrote the short fiction on the green pages. The images on the cover were collaged from The Basilica of the Sagrada Familia. Mely Kornfeld has granted permission for the use of her work in this publication, and “The Body at Work” has been released into the public domain. This issue was designed and edited by Tammy Nguyen with additional editing from Holly Greene. Production was led by Daniella Porras with assistance from Cheney Hester, Dylan Ng, Lena Weiman, Spencer Klink, and Lexi Radziner.
